Step 1 · Know Your User or Client
Whole deck (slides 1–29). Big idea: you cannot design a usable system until you understand the people who will use it — how they think, remember, see, feel and work.
1.1

What should you consider to create a usable system?

slide 3
In one line: Step 1 is a checklist of 7 things to understand about people before designing.
  1. Understand how people interact with computers.
  2. Understand the human characteristics important in design.
  3. Identify the user’s level of knowledge and experience.
  4. Identify the characteristics of the user’s needs, tasks and jobs.
  5. Identify the user’s psychological characteristics.
  6. Identify the user’s physical characteristics.
  7. Employ recommended methods for gaining understanding of users.
Example

Designing a banking app for a mall kiosk: Who uses it (elderly people? tourists?), what do they already know (smartphones?), what is their task (withdraw cash quickly), are they stressed (queue behind them), can they see small text, and how do we find all this out (visit the mall and watch people).

Memory hook

Think of the 7 points as a story about a person: How does she use it → What is she like as a human → What does she know → What does she do → How does she feel → What is her body like → How do we find out.

1.2

How people interact with computers — The Human Action Cycle

slide 4
In one line: Every interaction is a loop: Goal → Execute → Evaluate.
  • A goal is formed — what do I want to achieve?
  • An execution plan is created and implemented — decide the actions, then do them.
  • The resulting actions taken are evaluated — look at the result: did I reach my goal? If not, loop again.
Interactive example — click “Next stage”

Exam tip If asked “describe the Human Action Cycle”, write the 3 stages in order and give a one-line example for each.
1.3

Why people have trouble with computers

slide 5
In one line: Five design faults confuse people.
1. Too much flexibilityToo many functions and options. e.g. Word’s ribbon with 200 buttons for someone who only wants to type a letter.
2. Non-obvious designThe design is complex; you can’t tell how it works. e.g. a hidden gesture with no hint.
3. Fine distinctionsTiny differences between things. e.g. “Save” vs “Save As” vs “Save a Copy”.
4. Disparity in problem-solving strategiesUsers are not at the same level; they solve problems differently. e.g. an expert types a shortcut, a beginner looks for a menu.
5. Design inconsistencyThe same action has different names. e.g. save, keep, list, write — all meaning “save”.
Memory hook
F · N · F · D · I

“Funny Nerds Find Difficult Interfaces” → Flexibility, Non-obvious, Fine distinctions, Disparity, Inconsistency.

1.7

Human characteristic B — Memory

slides 9–10
In one line: Short-term memory is tiny and fast to forget, so design to minimise what users must remember.
Short-term (working) memoryLasts 10 to 30 seconds
Holds 3 to 4 items
Long-term (knowledge) memoryActive vocabulary (words you can recall): 2,000–3,000 words
Passive vocabulary (words you can recognise): 10,000 words

Notice: we recognise far more than we can recall (10,000 vs 3,000). That is why menus (recognition) are easier than typing commands (recall).

Guidelines (minimise the need for memory)

  • Present information in an organised, structured, familiar and meaningful way.
  • Place all information needed for the task in close physical proximity.
  • Give the user control over the pace of information presentation.
  • Do not require working memory if reading speed is important.
  • Make important items unique or distinct.
Try it — test your short-term memory

Press start. A code appears for 4 seconds. Then type it.

Memory hook

Short-term: “3–4 items, 10–30 seconds” → like a tiny pocket that empties every half-minute. Long-term vocab: “Active 3 thousand, Passive 10 thousand” (2–3K / 10K).

Human characteristic D — Visual acuity (sharpness)

slides 12–13
In one line: The eye sees sharply only a small area at a time, so group the screen into visual chunks.
  • Visual acuity = the capacity of the eye to resolve detail.
  • Guideline: provide screen groupings that reflect visual chunks.
  • Guideline: patterns for fill-in areas (bars, circles…) must be chosen carefully to avoid visual distraction.

1.10

Human characteristic E — Information processing

slide 14
In one line: The brain has a slow “thinking” level and a fast “automatic” level — design so the fast level can do the work.
Higher level 🐢Lower level ⚡
Identified with working memory
Limited, slow and sequential (reading and understanding)
Performs reasoning and problem solving
Subject to interference		 Processes familiar information rapidly
Works in parallel with the higher level
Without effort

Guidelines

  • Provide visually distinctive screens to enhance lower-level processing.
  • Minimise interference — avoid designs that create conflicting mental processes.
Example — conflicting processes

Say the colour of each word (not the word):

RED   GREEN   YELLOW   BLUE

It feels slow because your fast level reads the word while your slow level tries to name the colour — they conflict. On a screen, a red “Continue” button or a green “Delete” button causes the same conflict.

1.11

Human characteristic F — Mental models (vs conceptual models)

slides 15–16
In one line: A mental model is what a person believes about how something works — design new systems to match what people already know.
🧠 Mental modelCaptures ideas in a problem domain in a person’s head. A representation of a person’s current understanding of something.
📐 Conceptual modelRepresents “concepts” (entities) and the relationships between them. A known data-modelling technique, together with logical and physical modelling.
  • Mental models are gradually developed to understand, explain, make decisions, do, or interact.
  • Learned mental models are applied to what is newly encountered.

Guidelines

  • Design new systems conforming to the user’s already learned mental models.
  • Provide design consistency and standards to help mental model development.
Example

The 🗑️ Trash/Recycle bin: people know a real bin — you throw things in and can take them back out until it’s emptied. The computer bin works the same way, so nobody needs to be taught. A shopping-cart icon works for the same reason.

1.12

Human characteristic G — Learning, and Skill

slides 17–18
In one line: Learning moves info from short-term to long-term memory; skill is doing the right actions, in the right order, precisely, with little effort.
📚 LearningThe process of encoding into long-term memory what is in short-term memory.

Guidelines:
  • Allow skills from one situation to be used in a similar one (transfer).
  • Provide complete and prompt feedback.
  • Provide phased learning — only what is needed at that stage.
🎯 SkillPerformance of actions in the correct time sequence with adequate precision.
Achieved by increasing mastery of a system; characterised by economy of effort for optimum efficiency.

Guideline: permit development of increasingly skilful performance.
Example

Learning: Ctrl+C / Ctrl+V works in every app → transfer. Duolingo gives instant ✔/✘ → prompt feedback, and unlocks one unit at a time → phased learning.
Skill: a beginner right-clicks → Copy; with practice they use Ctrl+C without thinking — same result, less effort.

1.13

Performance load

slide 19
In one line: The effort needed to do a task — reduce the thinking load and the movement load.
🧠 Reduce cognitive (thinking) load
  • Eliminate noise / unnecessary information
  • Properly format and group information
  • Use powers of recognition
  • Automate tasks that need extensive memory
✋ Reduce kinaesthetic (movement) load
  • Minimise number of steps
  • Minimise control actions and movements
  • Avoid repetitive tasks
Example

Checkout page: the site remembers your address (automates memory → less cognitive load) and lets you pay with one tap (fewer steps → less kinaesthetic load).

Memory hook

Cognitive = brain, Kinaesthetic = body (kinetic = movement).

User’s tasks and needs

slide 24
In one line: Must they use it? How often? How important is it? How much training?

  • Frequency of use — daily vs once a year.

Methods for gaining understanding of users

slide 29
In one line: Go there, talk, watch, learn the organisation, ask in writing.
  1. Visit user locations to understand the work environment.
  2. Talk directly with users about problems, difficulties, wishes, and what works well now.

Step 3 · Principles of Good Screen Design
Slides 30–56. Big idea: a good screen is simple, grouped, aligned, readable and consistent — so the eye finds things fast and the brain doesn’t have to work hard.
3.1

Unity vs Fragmentation

slide 30
In one line: Elements that are close together and similar in size look like one unit; elements spread far apart look broken into pieces.
Unity ✔ close, same size → one whole
Fragmentation ✘ pushed to the edges → 4 separate pieces

These are part of the aesthetic pairs of screen design (the deck also shows Predictable vs Spontaneous and Sequential vs Random on the slides just before 30). The good side of each pair is the one to aim for.

Memory hook

Unity = united family sitting together at one table. Fragmentation = fragments of a broken plate on the floor.

3.2

Simplicity vs Complexity — count the alignments

slides 31–32
In one line: A simple screen has few alignment points for the eye to process: the simple form has 4, the complex form has 9.
Why it matters

Each vertical line is a place where the eye has to “start again”. With 4 lines, the eye moves down neat columns. With 9, it zig-zags and the brain processes more. Same 8 fields + 2 buttons, very different effort.

Exam tip Remember the numbers: Simple = 4 alignments, Complex = 9 alignments.
3.3

Grouping elements — align, borders, background colour

slides 33–34
In one line: Make groups visible in 3 ways: aligning elements, line borders, and background colour.

Personal details (Name→Zip) form one group; payment (Dues, Pubs, Total) another; buttons sit below the payment group.

Memory hook

A-B-C of grouping: Align → Borders → Colour (background).

3.4

Perceptual principles and grouping

slide 35
In one line: Use the perception principles from Step 1 to build functional groups — combine them, but never let them fight.
  • Use visual organisation to create functional groupings: Proximity, Similarity, Closure, Matching patterns.
  • Combine principles in logical ways — e.g. proximity & similarity together.
  • Avoid principles that conflict.
Combined ✔

Close and same look → two obvious groups.

Conflict ✘

Proximity says “left / right”, similarity says “circles / squares” → the brain is confused.

3.6

Typography — purpose, size, style, defaults, consistency

slides 37–41
In one line: Fonts show organisation, importance, reading order and mood — use max 3 sizes, standard fonts, and a consistent hierarchy.

What a font’s characteristics can do

1. Communicate organisation of screen elements
2. Identify the most important elements
3. Establish a reading order
4. Create a particular mood

Font size rules

  • Use no more than three sizes (consider the “x” height).
  • Graphical systems: 12 pt for menus, 10 pt for windows.
  • Web pages: 12–14 pt body text, 18–36 pt titles and headings.
  • Line spacing: 1 to 1½ times the font size.
  • Never change established type sizes to squeeze in more text.
Playground — pick a use

Font styles

  • Italics → to call attention.
  • Bold → to call attention or create a hierarchy.
  • Underline → on Web pages, only to indicate a navigation link.

Defaults

  • Graphical OS: use the standard system fonts.
  • Web: use the default browser fonts.
  • Remember the user may change the fonts.

Consistency

  • Establish a consistent hierarchy and convention for typefaces, styles and sizes.
  • Decide on a font for each level of importance.
  • Communicate hierarchy with changes in Size, Weight, Colour.

3.7

Application screen elements — Window title & Web page title

slides 42–43
In one line: Every window and web page needs a clear title at the top.
🪟 Window title
  • All windows must have a title at the top.
  • Exception: windows containing messages.
  • Clearly and concisely describe the window’s purpose.
  • Spell out fully, in upper- or mixed-case.
🌐 Web page title
  • Titles in the browser bar and on the content page.
  • Both titles must be consistent.
  • Titles must be: Descriptive, Unique (meaningfully different from other pages), Concise.
Example

3.9

Lists

slides 46–47
In one line: Put related items in a vertical list with a heading, in a meaningful order.
  • Present a collection of related items in a list.
  • Use sentence or headline capitalisation consistently.
  • Provide a heading for each list.

3.10

Keying procedures — keystrokes, tabbing, manual tab vs auto skip

slides 48–51
In one line: Start the cursor in the first field, let users Tab manually; use auto skip only for experts, easy screens and always-full fields.
  • Keystrokes: don’t focus only on minimising keystrokes — also consider the keying rhythm and the goals of the system.
  • Tabbing: initially position the cursor in the first field where information can be entered. (Type your name, press Tab → next box.)
  • Define fields to permit manual tabbing.
  • Consider auto skip for: expert users, easily learned screens, screens with fields always completely filled.

Step 3 · The Web & Technology
Slides 57–87. Big idea: on the Web, content is king, people scan instead of read, and your design is limited by the hardware, browsers and connection speed people actually have.
W.1

Web user interaction characteristics & interaction styles

slides 57–58
In one line: Web users want content, have a goal, scan instead of reading line by line, and change purpose often.
Characteristics
  • The most important Web commodity is content
  • Behaviour is often goal-driven
  • Reading is no longer linear
  • People scan and select
  • Frequent switching of purpose
Interaction styles
  • Browsing — looking around without a specific target
  • Information finding — looking for one specific thing
  • Information gathering — collecting many pieces (research)
Example

Scrolling Instagram’s explore page = browsing. Checking the opening hours of Dubai Mall = information finding. Comparing 5 laptops for a purchase = information gathering.

Memory hook

Characteristics = “Content, Goal, Non-linear, Scan, Switch” → “Cats Get Naps, Sleep, Snore”. Styles = B · F · G: Browse, Find, Gather.

Intranet & Extranet design guidelines

slides 67–68
In one line: Intranet (inside the company) = one home page with directory, search and news, a 4-area layout, and its own visual style.

  • Home page layout: top horizontal bar, left column, middle area, right column.

  • Orient the website toward tasks.

  • Provide links to the public Internet site.

W.11

Other Web considerations — Downloading

slides 78–79
In one line: A page should download in no more than 8–10 seconds (at 28.8 Kbps), and before a file download tell the user everything they need to decide.
🐢 Techniques that slow downloads (minimise them)
  • Long pages
  • Large headings
  • Numerous or large graphics and images (GIF is smaller than JPEG — per the slide)
  • Animation
  • Excessive amount of colour
  • Excess use of frames
📦 Before a download, tell the user
  • Program or document description
  • Type of download
  • Size of download
  • Download version
  • Estimated loading time
  • Special operating requirements
Calculator — will my page pass the 8–10 second rule at 28.8 Kbps?
Page size: 30 KB

Time = size in KB × 8 ÷ 28.8 Kbps. So the 8–10 s limit means a page of roughly 29–36 KB on that connection.
